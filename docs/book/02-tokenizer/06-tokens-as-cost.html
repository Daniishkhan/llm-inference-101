The problem: the same sentence, different bills

A model is paid for, timed and limited in tokens. A service bills per token, a reply arrives one token at a time, and a context window holds so many tokens and no more. So the first question about a text is not how long it is, but how many tokens the tokenizer makes of it. Here is one sentence, The train to the airport leaves every twenty minutes, in six languages, counted by the oracle, and then one word of it, "airport", split into its tokens in five of them.

The program's first lines build tiktoken's GPT-2 encoding from the two files in data/gpt2/. They are chapter 4's lines (tiktoken, the oracle), unchanged: type them with the page open, and the rest from memory. This chapter has two programs that start this way, and this is the first. The six sentences are about the same length in characters, except Korean and Japanese, which pack a word into one or two characters. Yet Greek and Russian take 58 tokens to English's 10. A service that bills per token bills almost six times as much to read the Russian sentence, and a model that writes it takes almost six times as many steps.

First principle

A model does a fixed amount of work for each token: one row of its embedding table in, one pass through every layer, and, when it writes, one score for every token of the vocabulary out. It never sees characters or words. So whatever costs money, time or memory in an engine is counted in tokens, and the tokenizer decides how many there are before the model sees anything.

Two things make the difference, and you have met both. The first is bytes per character. UTF-8 spends one byte on an English letter, two on a Greek or Cyrillic one, three on a Korean or Japanese character (chapter 1). Before a single merge, the Russian sentence is 97 bytes to English's 53.

The second is which merges were learned. GPT-2's merges were learned from its training text, a large collection of web pages that is overwhelmingly English. The pairs most frequent in English were merged first, and whole English words became single tokens. Pairs of Cyrillic or Greek bytes were rare, so few of them earned a merge. The second half of the output shows it, one word in each script. " airport", with its space, is one token. " aéroport" is four: é is two bytes, and the pieces around it are English fragments. The Greek and Russian words fall apart into tokens of one or two bytes, and the Korean one into the space, three single bytes and two more pieces. Those words take more tokens than they have characters.

Look at b'\xce' then b'\xb4' in the Greek word, and b'\xd0\xbe\xd0' in the Russian one. Those tokens end in the middle of a character: a merge knows only bytes, so nothing stops it from joining the end of one character to the start of the next. That is why a token decoded on its own can show "�" (chapter 1).

Analogy

A parcel service that charges per box. Its packer has boxes made to measure for the common objects of one country: a kettle goes in one box. An object from elsewhere has no box of its own, so it is cut into pieces, each in a small standard box, and the same kettle made abroad ships in six boxes at six times the price. Where it breaks: a packer could learn new boxes as orders come in, but a tokenizer's merges are fixed at training, and every text is packed with them for ever. And the model's work per box is not quite constant: late in a long text, each token's attention looks back over more tokens, so a late token costs a little more than an early one.

Characters per token

A number people quote is characters per token: a text's length in characters divided by its number of tokens. For English prose and GPT-2 it is often given as about four. Here it is on three texts written for this chapter: a paragraph of plain prose about a lighthouse keeper, the same paragraph in capitals, and a line of phone numbers and times (Call 0161 496 0738 or 0161 496 0912 before 17:45 on 03/11/2026.). From the oracle, counted with the encoding of same_sentence.py:

TextCharactersTokensCharacters per token

The same, in capitals6562262.90

4.52 on the paragraph: a little better than four, because it is plain prose of common words, and most of them, with their leading space, are single tokens. In capitals it takes 226 tokens against 145, because capitalised words were rare in the training text and few were merged. The numbers are worse still, at 2.42: the split puts each run of digits in a chunk of its own, and most runs of four digits, even " 2026", take two tokens.

So the ratio belongs to a text and a tokenizer together, not to a language. Four is a fair first guess for English prose with GPT-2; for code, numbers, capitals or another script, measure. The sentences of the first program give 5.3 for English and 0.9 for Russian.

Trap

Every model family has its own tokenizer, and counts do not carry over. A budget worked out with GPT-2's tokenizer is wrong for a model with another vocabulary, sometimes badly for languages other than English. Count with the tokenizer of the model you will call.

Paying per token

Services that run models price them in dollars per million tokens, with two prices: one for input tokens (the prompt you send) and one for output tokens (the reply). The output price is usually the higher, and the next section shows why: the prompt is read in one go, while each output token is a step of its own.

Take two example prices, invented for this chapter and not those of any real service: $0.40 per million input tokens and $1.60 per million output tokens. A request sends 1,500 tokens and gets 300 back. Each part costs its tokens times its price, divided by a million:

About a tenth of a cent, and $1,080 for a million such requests. The reply is a sixth of the tokens and almost half of the bill.

Now suppose the same conversation were in Russian, and the first program's ratio of 5.8 held throughout (one sentence is not a law, but it is the right size for GPT-2). The request becomes 8,700 tokens in and 1,740 out, and every line of the table grows by the same factor: the same conversation, model and prices, and a bill almost six times as large, decided before the model saw a word.

Per document, input 20,000 × 0.40 / 1,000,000 = $0.008 and output 500 × 1.60 / 1,000,000 = $0.0008: $0.0088. For 10,000 documents, $88, of which $80 is input. With a long prompt and a short reply, the input dominates, although each output token costs four times as much.

Tokens and latency

Time is the other cost of a token. An engine handles a request in two phases:

  • Prefill. The engine has every token of the prompt, so it runs them through the model together, in parallel. A thousand-token prompt takes far less than a thousand times as long as a one-token prompt. Prefill ends with the first token of the reply, so it sets the time to first token.
  • Decode. Each further token depends on the one before, so the model makes them one at a time: one step, a full pass through the model, per output token. A 300-token reply is 300 steps, and the length of a step is the time per output token.

With example timings, invented for the arithmetic, of 50 ms of prefill and 20 ms per step: the English reply of 300 tokens takes 50 + 300 × 20 = 6,050 ms, and the Russian one of 1,740 tokens 50 + 1,740 × 20 = 34,850 ms. Six seconds against thirty-five, for the same answer.

Analogy

Reading a letter and writing the reply. You take in a page at a glance, so a long letter reads almost as fast as a short one; but you write the reply a word at a time, rereading what you have so far before each next word. Where it breaks: a model would really reread the whole conversation at every step, unless it kept what it had already worked out about each token. That store is the KV cache.

Two limits are in tokens too. A model's context window is the most tokens it can hold, prompt and reply together; GPT-2's is 1,024, which holds the English sentence 102 times (1,024 // 10) and the Russian one 17 times (1,024 // 58). And the KV cache, where the engine keeps every layer's keys and values for every token so far, grows by a fixed amount per token: 128 KiB for a model shaped like Llama 3.1 8B (Module 1, chapter 6). At that size the English sentence holds 1.25 MiB of cache and the Russian one 7.25 MiB. Module 5 builds the cache. A longer tokenization means more steps, more memory, and fewer requests on the same GPU at once.

How big should a vocabulary be?

Why not learn more merges, so that every word of every language gets a token? Because each token in the vocabulary costs something too. You can see both sides with GPT-2's files: keep only the first m merges, which is the tokenizer that training would have produced had it stopped after m rounds, and count the tokens of one text. GPT-2's ids follow the order its merges were learned, so the tokenizer of the first m merges is the oracle built from only the ranks below 256 + m: the 256 bytes and those merges. The table also sizes the embedding table, which holds a row of numbers for every token of the vocabulary: 768 numbers per row in GPT-2 small. From the oracle, on one sentence of 175 characters (In winter the storms came in from the west, and for weeks at a time the supply boat could not land, so he kept a shelf of tinned food for the nights when the generator failed.):

MergesVocabularyTokensNumbers in the tableMB as float32
0256175196,6080.8
1,0001,25664964,6083.9
5,0005,256494,036,60816.1
20,00020,2564015,556,60862.2
50,00050,2564038,596,608154.4

With no merges every byte is a token: 175 for 175 characters. A thousand merges cut that to 64, five thousand to 49, twenty thousand to 40, and the last thirty thousand do nothing for this sentence: they serve rarer words and other scripts. Each merge helps less text than the one before, because the common pairs went first.

The table, meanwhile, grows in a straight line. GPT-2's 50,257 tokens (256 bytes, 50,000 merges and <|endoftext|>) at 768 numbers each are about 38.6 million numbers, nearly a third of GPT-2 small's roughly 124 million parameters. And the vocabulary is paid for twice. At the input, the table is only a lookup by id. At the output, the model scores every token of the vocabulary at every step and turns the scores into probabilities with the softmax: for GPT-2, 50,257 scores of 768 multiplications each, about 38.6 million multiply-adds per token written. A rare token, seen a handful of times in training, also gets only a handful of chances to learn a good row.

So a vocabulary's size is a trade: fewer tokens per text, and so fewer steps and less cache, against a bigger table and a bigger softmax at every step. GPT-2 chose 50,257. Several newer open models use vocabularies of more than 100,000 tokens.

Measuring a tokenizer's speed

A tokenizer's speed is quoted in characters per second (or bytes, or tokens: say which). Time it on a long text, because a short one measures mostly the cost of the call, and many times, because one timing lies (Module 1, chapter 1): the first call can pay one-time costs, and any call can be slowed by something else on the machine. Report the median.

The program makes a text of its own, about 96,000 characters of random words, times one call with time.perf_counter_ns around tiktoken's encode_ordinary, then 21 calls with the standard library's timeit.repeat. Its last lines time something else, for the next section: a Python loop that only adds up the text's bytes. It is the chapter's second program that starts with the oracle's lines; type those with the page open again. One run on the M5; yours will differ:

Python you need

timeit.repeat(work, timer=time.perf_counter_ns, number=1, repeat=21) calls work() 21 times and returns 21 durations, read from the clock you pass, here in nanoseconds; number=1 means one call per duration. It switches the garbage collector off while it times. statistics.median is the middle value.

Why tiktoken is fast

tiktoken's Python package is a thin layer over a library written in Rust, a language compiled to machine code before it runs, as C is. One call to encode_ordinary crosses from Python into Rust, and the whole job happens there: the split, by a regular expression engine written in Rust, and the merges of every chunk. It is numpy's bargain (Module 1, chapter 6): one call, much work in compiled code, no bytecode per step, and each byte of the text kept as one byte.

Python keeps the same bytes less compactly. A bytes object is one byte per byte, plus 33 bytes of header. Turn it into a list of ids and each item is a pointer, an 8-byte address of an int object: sys.getsizeof of the list of the program's 96,553 byte values divided by its length is 8.0. The int objects themselves cost almost nothing more, because CPython keeps one shared object for each of the ints from −5 to 256, for the whole run: every 101 in the list is the same object, as id() shows, and the whole list points at only 27 objects, one for each different byte in the text. The real price is not the memory but the steps. Every item read, every addition and every comparison is several bytecode instructions, each fetched and dispatched by the interpreter.

The last two lines of tokenizer_speed.py put a number on that: the cheapest thing Python can do with a text, one addition per byte in a loop, costs about 8.6 ns per byte. tiktoken does the whole tokenization, the split and every merge, for about twice that. A Python encoder does far more per byte than one addition: it makes a list of ids, builds a tuple for each pair of neighbours and looks it up in a dict of ranks, and each of those steps goes through the interpreter. In Rust the same steps are a few machine instructions each. That is where the time goes in principle. How it adds up on a real text, how much faster tiktoken is than your encoder and what apply_merges_fast saves over apply_merges when the chunks are short, is the Experiment's to measure; this section gives you the material for a prediction, not its answer.

In a real engine

vLLM counts in tokens, with the model's own tokenizer (its Hugging Face tokenizer, by default). Its OpenAI-compatible server reports in each response's usage field the prompt_tokens and completion_tokens (and their sum, total_tokens): the two numbers a bill per million multiplies by its two prices. Its limits are in tokens too. The model's maximum length (--max-model-len) is the context window, prompt and reply together, and a request whose prompt does not fit is refused with an error. The batch's token budget (--max-num-batched-tokens) caps how many tokens one step of the scheduler may process across all its requests; Module 9 builds a scheduler with such a budget. A tokenizer that makes 58 tokens where another makes 10 spends that budget, and the KV cache behind it, almost six times as fast.

Check yourself

The English and Russian sentences have 53 and 52 characters and take 10 and 58 tokens. Give the two reasons, each with a number from the first program.

Answer

Bytes per character: Cyrillic letters take two bytes, so 97 bytes against 53. Merges: GPT-2's were learned from mostly English text, so English's 53 bytes shrink to 10 tokens and Russian's 97 only to 58.

Roughly how many GPT-2 tokens is 12,000 characters of ordinary English prose, and when would you distrust the estimate?

Answer

At four to four and a half characters per token, about 2,700 to 3,000. Distrust it for code, numbers, capitals, names or another script, or for a model with another tokenizer: then count.

At the example prices ($0.40 and $1.60 per million), what does a request with a 4,000-token prompt and a 1,000-token reply cost?

Answer

4,000 × 0.40 / 1,000,000 = $0.0016 plus 1,000 × 1.60 / 1,000,000 = $0.0016: $0.0032. A fifth of the tokens, half the bill.

Which makes a reply take longer to finish, doubling the prompt or doubling the reply? Why?

Answer

Doubling the reply. The prompt is processed in parallel during prefill, while every reply token is a decode step of its own. A longer prompt lengthens the time to first token and the cache, but adds no steps.

A vocabulary of 128,000 tokens with 4,096 numbers per row: how many numbers is the embedding table, and how many bytes in bf16 (2 bytes per number)?

Answer

128,000 × 4,096 = 524,288,000 numbers; at 2 bytes each, about 1.05 GB. And the softmax scores 128,000 tokens at every step.

Why does a tokenizer benchmark need a warm-up, many calls and the median, and why must it name its text?

Answer

The first call can pay one-time costs and any call can be slowed by something else, so one timing can be far from typical; the median of many shrugs off the odd slow call. Speed depends on the text's chunks and their merges, so a figure without its text cannot be compared.

Why is tiktoken fast? Name two things a Python encoder pays for at every step that tiktoken does not.

Answer

Its split and merges run in compiled Rust, inside one call from Python, with the text's bytes kept as bytes. A Python encoder pays the interpreter for every step: several bytecode instructions for each list read, each tuple it builds and each dict lookup. And its ids are 8-byte pointers to int objects rather than the bytes themselves.

A list of 96,553 byte values takes about 8 bytes per item. Why not 28, the size of an int?

Answer

A list holds pointers, 8 bytes each, not the ints themselves. And CPython keeps one shared object for each int from −5 to 256, so every 101 in the list points at the same object and the list adds no new ints at all.
